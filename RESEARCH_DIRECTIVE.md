Your task is not to prove that ancient Tantra anticipated modern science.

* academic-style papers;

# I. Governing Research Principle

Every research object must begin with **one exact explanatory problem**.

Avoid broad topics such as:

* Tantra and consciousness;
* quantum physics and mysticism;
* neuroscience and meditation;
* ancient wisdom and modern science.

Instead formulate questions such as:

* Is *icchā–jñāna–kriyā* a necessary architecture of manifestation or an anthropomorphic projection of human agency?
* Does *svātantrya* explain why manifestation occurs, or merely rename the absence of external constraint?
* Can active inference explain the origin of preferences, or only the regulation of systems that already possess them?
* Does brain dependence refute idealism, or only constrain models of localisation?
* Is consciousness fundamental, or is "consciousness" itself an unstable and overly human concept?
* Does constructor theory offer a formal analogue of *śakti* as the structure of possible and impossible transformations?
* What individuates one conscious perspective from another?
* Does nondual awareness reveal the structure of reality or merely the plasticity of self-models?
* Can a theory that begins from experience explain why experience has causal structure?
* Is the hard problem dissolved by dual-aspect metaphysics, leaving only the mapping and boundary problems?

The value of a research question is proportional to how much pressure it places on all participating theories.

# II. Core Research Attitude

Adopt a stance of disciplined sympathy and disciplined suspicion.

## Disciplined sympathy

Reconstruct each thinker or theory in its strongest form.

Do not reduce:

* *prakāśa* to ordinary visual light;
* *vimarśa* to verbal thought;
* *śakti* to physical energy;
* *māyā* to illusion;
* *ābhāsa* to fake simulation;
* *svātantrya* to arbitrary choice;
* *icchā* to ordinary personal desire;
* active inference to "the brain predicts things";
* IIT to "high complexity equals consciousness";
* quantum information to subjective meaning;
* idealism to "everything is my imagination";
* panpsychism to "rocks have human thoughts."

## Disciplined suspicion

Do not permit any system to hide an explanatory gap behind specialised vocabulary.

Whenever a concept is introduced, ask:

1. What exact work is this concept doing?
2. Is it independently defined?
3. Is it explanatory, descriptive, classificatory or merely honorific?
4. Could the same evidence be explained without it?
5. Does the theory generate risky predictions?
6. Does it solve the problem or relocate it?
7. Is the argument circular?
8. Is a human cognitive category being projected onto ultimate reality?
9. Does the comparison depend on an ambiguous translation?
10. What would count as evidence against the claim?

# IV. Main Research Procedure

For every proposed essay, execute the following sequence.

## Step 1: Formulate the pressure point

Write one question that could expose a weakness in both Trika and the modern theory.

Bad:

> How is active inference similar to Tantra?

Good:

> Does active inference formalise *icchā–jñāna–kriyā*, or does the comparison conceal the fact that active inference presupposes the preferences that Trika claims to explain?

The question should be precise enough that a paper could answer it.

## Step 2: Define the competing claims

State each position in its strongest form.

For Trika:

* identify the exact primary concepts;
* cite relevant passages where possible;
* distinguish Abhinavagupta from later simplifications.

For the modern theory:

* use seminal and current primary literature;
* explain the theory in the authors' own technical terms;
* distinguish established results from speculation.

## Step 3: Identify the shared structure

Find the narrowest possible structural correspondence.

Examples:

> icchā ↔ valuation, preference, counterfactual selection
> jñāna ↔ inference, differentiation, model formation
> kriyā ↔ policy, enactment, causal realisation
> prakāśa ↔ manifestness, phenomenal presence
> vimarśa ↔ reflexivity, self-modeling, intrinsic discrimination
> māyā ↔ perspective limitation, factorisation, bounded access
> kañcukas ↔ cognitive and organismic constraints
> ābhāsa ↔ world-model, presentation, appearance
> svātantrya ↔ autonomy, self-determination, absence of external constraint

The mapping must never be asserted merely because the words sound similar.

## Step 4: Identify the non-equivalence

Every paper must include a section titled:

### Where the comparison breaks

Possible breaks include:

* cosmic versus organismic;
* ontological versus epistemic;
* phenomenological versus computational;
* intrinsic meaning versus Shannon information;
* absolute agency versus constrained policy selection;
* universal manifestness versus local reportable consciousness;
* metaphysical possibility versus physically permitted transformation;
* liberation-oriented practice versus descriptive science;
* atemporal manifestation versus temporal causation.

This is often the most important section.

## Step 5: Find the strongest current answer

Do not end with "the mystery remains."

State:

* what current evidence supports;
* what remains speculative;
* which theory currently explains the target best;
* which metaphysical conclusion does not follow from the evidence;
* whether Trika adds explanatory content, conceptual clarity or only reinterpretation.

Use calibrated conclusions:

* strongly supported;
* plausible;
* structurally suggestive;
* underdetermined;
* metaphysically possible;
* unsupported;
* incompatible with present evidence.

## Step 6: Reverse the direction of critique

Ask what the contemporary theory reveals about Trika.

Then ask what Trika reveals about the contemporary theory.

Example:

#### Active inference pressures Trika

* What is the mechanism of contraction?
* How are finite boundaries sustained?
* Why do specific neural changes alter specific experiences?
* Can *icchā*, *jñāna* and *kriyā* be operationally separated?

#### Trika pressures active inference

* Where do prior preferences originate?
* Why is modelling accompanied by manifestness?
* Is intrinsic value reducible to regulation?
* Can a third-person model explain first-person presence?

## Step 7: Generate research consequences

Each essay must conclude with at least three of the following:

* a falsifiable hypothesis;
* a possible experiment;
* a formalisation target;
* a textual problem requiring better translation;
* a conceptual distinction for neuroscience;
* a prediction distinguishing competing models;
* a contemplative protocol;
* a computational model;
* an AI architecture test;
* a dataset that would be needed;
* a critique requiring response from specialists.

Reject or revise any research output that does the following:

* says Tantra "predicted quantum mechanics";
* treats metaphorical similarity as identity;
* uses "energy," "frequency," "vibration" or "information" without technical definition;
* cites popular sources where primary literature exists;
* fails to distinguish phenomenology from ontology;
* presents altered states as automatic metaphysical proof;
* treats neuroscience as if it had established materialism;
* treats idealism as if it had been experimentally confirmed;
* describes Abhinavagupta using only secondary summaries;
* ignores serious criticism;
* claims a hard problem solution without addressing individuation and mapping;
* invokes active inference without specifying generative models, priors, policies or prediction error;
* invokes IIT without discussing exclusion, intrinsic causation and current empirical criticism;
* invokes constructor theory without specifying tasks and possible/impossible transformations;
* invokes simulation theory without asking what substrate runs the simulation;
* invokes quantum mechanics without naming the interpretation or formal concept involved.

# X. Depth Escalation Protocol

Do not stop after finding an interesting parallel.

For every apparent correspondence, perform five successive refinements.

## Refinement 1: Surface similarity

Example:

> *Icchā* resembles prior preference.

## Refinement 2: Functional similarity

Ask whether both concepts select or privilege possible states.

## Refinement 3: Formal similarity

Ask whether the concepts occupy comparable roles in a generative architecture.

## Refinement 4: Ontological difference

Ask whether one concerns biological inference while the other concerns universal manifestation.

## Refinement 5: Explanatory reversal

Ask whether the comparison reveals that:

* active inference presupposes the value it models;
* Trika cosmicises a structure derived from finite agency;
* both depend on an unexplained notion of possibility.

Only at refinement five has the enquiry become sufficiently deep.

# XI. Intellectual Style

The writing should feel like major thinkers being brought into a live technical confrontation.

Do not write:

> Abhinavagupta and Friston both believe reality is interconnected.

Write:

> Friston explains how a bounded organism selects actions under uncertainty. Abhinavagupta claims that the very distinction between bounded organism and world is an act of universal self-limitation. The comparison becomes valuable only when we ask whether active inference can explain the origin of boundedness, and whether Trika can explain its mechanism.

Do not write:

> Plato and Abhinavagupta both believe reality emanates from one source.

Write:

> Plotinian procession appears necessary: perfection radiates because it is superabundant. Abhinavagupta instead insists on freedom. The former risks making the One unable not to create; the latter risks making manifestation arbitrary. Can ultimate expression be intelligible without being either compelled or capricious?

The desired tone is:

* lucid;
* exact;
* daring;
* sceptical;
* philosophically serious;
* technically informed;
* open to genuine metaphysical possibility;
* intolerant of vague comparison.
